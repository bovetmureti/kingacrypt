Why this practice exists

Someone can copy a ciphertext today and open it when the maths changes.

The habit has a name — harvest now, decrypt later. It does not wait for a quantum computer on Kimathi Street. It waits for any archive that must still be secret in fifteen years.

Kenya has not written post-quantum rules. Banks still spend on SOCs, DPA 2019, and CBK guidance. Payment rails still speak to M-Pesa. Government still runs public-key infrastructure designed for RSA. The work is to attach cryptographic honesty to those programmes, not to wait for a circular from the centre.

Oxford and Palo Alto already sell the libraries and the dashboards. Nairobi already has firms that hold the bank retainer. Almost nobody will sit in a Friday change window on a Kenyan core-banking host and swap the key exchange without taking Saturday down.

That seat is the job. Kinga is Kiswahili for shield. The rest is implementation.

A Tuesday, not a white paper

The night batch still signs with a certificate nobody has rotated since 2019.

Picture a payments switch in Nairobi. The edge looks modern. The SOC is staffed. Then you ask where the ISO messages are signed, and someone opens a ticket from three owners ago. The library is in a JAR. The password is in a wiki. The certificate expires on a Sunday in November. Nobody wants to be the person who restarts the host.

That is where post-quantum work actually lives. Not in a slide about Shor’s algorithm. In the service account, the callback to mobile money, the VPN the agency bankers still use, the backup tape that holds seven years of customer files because the act says so.

A KingaScan is us walking that building with your engineers — asking the night-shift people, not only the people who own the deck — and writing down what would still hurt if the ciphertext were copied tonight.

Briefings

We publish the thinking, the way a serious cyber house does.

Not a threat-intel feed. Short notes you can send to a colleague who still thinks this is a 2035 problem.

01

Harvest-now is a records problem, not a physics problem

If a loan file, a health record, or a land transaction must stay closed for ten or twenty years, the attacker does not need a quantum computer today. They need a copy and patience. That is already true for any East African archive sitting on RSA. The first useful artefact is not a quantum roadmap. It is a list of which stores would still matter if they leaked tonight.

02

Kenya has no PQC law. Attach the work anyway.

CBK is building sector cyber capacity. DPA 2019 already cares about how long personal data stays identifiable. NPKI is the country’s public-key spine. A KingaScan is written as an annex to those programmes so it does not die as “the quantum paper.”

03

Hybrid first. Cut over when it is boring.

ML-KEM beside today’s TLS. Old and new running together. The firms that break production are the ones who treat Friday as a ceremony. We treat Friday as a rehearsal that can roll back before the night batch starts.

The algorithms we implement

Public NIST standards. Local change windows.

These are the names your auditor will ask for. We use the FIPS titles, and we will still say Kyber or Dilithium in the room if that is the language your engineers already have.

FIPS 203

ML-KEM

The lock on the conversation. Formerly CRYSTALS-Kyber. Usually the first hybrid we add next to today’s TLS.

FIPS 204

ML-DSA

Who is allowed to say a payment or a document is real. Formerly CRYSTALS-Dilithium.

FIPS 205

SLH-DSA

A conservative hash-based spare. Formerly SPHINCS+. Not always the first move.

Questions people actually ask

Straight answers, including the unflattering ones.

Do we need this if Kenya has no PQC law yet?

The law is late. The copies of your ciphertext do not wait for it. If a record must stay closed for a decade, the conversation is already due. We attach the work to DPA, CBK, and the programmes you already fund.

Will this break M-Pesa or core banking?

Not if we do it in the order the scan writes. Hybrid first, then cut over when the new path is dull. A Friday night cut with no map is how outages happen.

Are you competing with our current cyber firm?

No. They keep the retainer, the SOC, the pentest cycle. We come in for cryptography they do not staff. Several first engagements will arrive through a firm you already know.

Do you sell an HSM or write a new algorithm?

Neither. We implement NIST standards with validated libraries. Hardware, if you need it, comes from a partner such as a Utimaco or Thales channel.

What does a first conversation cost?

The first note is to see whether a scan is the right step. Pricing depends on how many systems are in scope. We will not invent a number before we have heard what you run.
